## Two-minute warning vs Two-Minute Timeout

- **NFL:** two-minute warning, defined in 3-41. A suspension of play at the two-minute mark of each half. Several rules key off it: the fumble rule (8-7-6), no coach challenges (15-1), the replay/nullified-foul runoff (4-7).
- **NCAA:** the **Two-Minute Timeout** (3-3-5), new in 2024. It is a clock stoppage at 2:00 of the 2nd and 4th quarters. Several clock rules key off it: first downs stop the clock (3-3-2-e-1), out-of-bounds starts on the snap (3-3-2-d-2), and the foul runoff (3-4-4). Since 2026 the play clock freezes during a replay review after the Two-Minute Timeout (3-2-4-c-12). Before 2024 there was no college two-minute stoppage. Don't apply these rules to an earlier season.
- **No two-minute fumble rule in college.** The NFL rule (8-7-6) limits recovery and advance after the warning. NCAA has only the fourth-down fumble rule (7-2-2-a Exc. 2).

## 10-second runoffs

**NFL**
- **Replay or nullified foul after the two-minute warning** (Rule 4, Section 7). Suppose a reversal, or a foul called with time in and then picked up, means the correct ruling would not have stopped the clock. Officials then run 10 seconds and reset the play clock to 30, and the ball is put in play on the ready signal. **Neither team can decline the runoff.** Either team can prevent it with a timeout.
- **Fouls that stop the clock** in the final minute of a half (Rule 4, Section 7): a runoff applies. Which team may decline, and the exact fouls covered, are unverified here — check 4-7-1.
- **Excess timeout charged to the offense** that stops the clock: 10 seconds off if the defense chooses, play clock to 30, clock starts on the ready signal. A half can end on the runoff (4-5-1, Notes 3–4). A foul on the same down prevents the injury runoff.

**NCAA** (summary at 3-4-5: injury, helmet off, foul, replay)
- **Foul runoff** (3-4-4) applies when all of these hold:
  - the clock is running;
  - it is after the Two-Minute Timeout of the 2nd or 4th quarter;
  - there has been no change of possession;
  - either team commits a foul that stops the clock immediately and the penalty is accepted.
- Fouls covered: false start, encroachment and other fouls that prevent the snap; grounding to stop the clock; an incomplete illegal forward pass; a backward pass thrown out of bounds to stop the clock; any foul meant to stop the clock.
- How it works:
  - It is the **offended team's option**. Declining the yardage declines the runoff.
  - The fouling team can avoid it with a timeout.
  - It doesn't apply to offsetting fouls, or to fouls that don't stop the clock, such as illegal formation.
  - With a runoff, the clock starts on the ready signal. Without one, it starts on the snap (3-4-4-c).
- **Injury** (3-3-6-f) and **helmet coming off through play** (3-3-10-b) can bring runoffs too. **2025 injury rule:** an injury stoppage after the ball is spotted is charged to the team as a timeout, or is a 5-yard delay penalty if it has none left. This targets feigned injuries; for exact triggers check 3-3-6.

## Untimed downs (extension of a period)

A half (NFL) or a quarter (NCAA) is extended by one untimed down when time expires during a down and any of the following is true. Extensions repeat until a down is free of these conditions.

| NFL (4-8-2) | NCAA (3-2-3) |
|---|---|
| Accepted **defensive** live-ball foul | Accepted live-ball foul by **either team**, 2nd or 4th quarter. If the offense fouls and the penalty includes loss of down, the offended team may refuse the extension (3-2-3-a-1) |
| Certain offensive fouls, at the defense's option (see 4-8-2-b) | Offsetting fouls (a-2) |
| Fair catch situations, including a fair catch kick election (d–f) | Inadvertent whistle (a-3) |
| A safety caused by a foul on the last play counts (g) | Fair catch kick elected after a fair catch (a-4) |
| Double foul (h) | Touchdown on the last play: extended for the try (3-2-3-c), unless the try can't affect the result (8-3-2-a) |

Common error: assuming the NFL extends for any offensive foul. It does so only for the listed cases, and an accepted offensive foul often ends the half. NCAA extends for accepted fouls by either team, unless the offended team declines when the offense's foul carries loss of down.

Tries are untimed in both codes, so a touchdown as time expires is followed by a try only when the try can still matter. NFL: no try after a winning touchdown at the end of regulation (since 2018) or after a touchdown that ends overtime. NCAA: the try is skipped when it can't affect the outcome (8-3-2-a).

## NFL overtime (Rule 16)

| | Regular season (16-1-3) | Postseason (16-1-4) |
|---|---|---|
| Length | One 10-minute period | 15-minute periods, as many as needed |
| Possessions | Both teams get the chance to possess, unless the kicking team scores a safety on the receiving team's first possession. After both have had it (or the first possession is scoreless), the next score wins | Same possession rule (in effect since 2022) |
| Ties | Possible if still tied at the end of the period (16-1-3-d) | None; play continues |
| Timeouts | Two per team (16-1-3-e) | Three per half (16-1-4-g) |
| Challenges | None; replay booth only (16-1-5-d) | Same |

- Before 2025, a touchdown on the first regular-season possession ended the game. The 2025 change gave both teams a possession in the regular season too, matching the postseason rule from 2022. The 10-minute regular-season period predates 2025; it was shortened from 15 in 2017 (unverified — check that season's book).
- A defensive touchdown on the first possession ends the game. If the first team kicks a field goal, the second team wins with a touchdown, and a field goal ties it to continue (footballzebras.com analysis of the 2025 rules).
- A team that wins the toss and wants the second possession elects to kick.
- Whether an onside kick is allowed in regular-season overtime under 2026 rules: unverified — check 16-1-3 and 6-1-6.

## NCAA overtime (3-1-3)

- **Format:** after a tie, a coin toss. The winner **may not defer** and chooses either offense/defense or the end of the field (3-1-3-b). The loser gets the remaining choice and gets first choice in even-numbered periods (c).
- **Possession series** (d–e):
  - Each team snaps from the opponent's **25-yard line**, and keeps the ball until it scores or fails to make a first down.
  - If the defense takes the ball away, the ball stays live until dead, but the original offense can't earn a first down if it regains possession.
  - There is no game clock.
- **Scoring by period** (e–f):
  1. First overtime: normal possessions; after a touchdown, either a kick or a two-point try.
  2. Second overtime: normal possessions; after a touchdown the two-point try is **mandatory**. A one-point attempt is not illegal, but it can't score.
  3. Third overtime and later: no possessions. Each team gets **one two-point play from the 3**.
- **Ending:** both teams get equal possession series, unless the defense scores during the opponent's series (other than on a try). A defensive score in the first half of a period therefore ends the game.
- **Fouls after a change of possession** (g):
  - Penalties are declined by rule, except flagrant, unsportsmanlike, dead-ball personal fouls and live-ball fouls treated as dead-ball. Those are enforced on the next play.
  - A score by the fouling team is cancelled.
  - If both teams foul and the defense hadn't fouled before the change, the fouls cancel and the down isn't replayed.
- **Timeouts** (h): one per team in the first overtime, one in the second, then one per team from the third until the game ends. Timeouts left over from regulation don't carry over, and unused overtime timeouts don't carry to the next period. After the second overtime, there's a two-minute break if no media timeout is taken.
- **History:** the mandatory two-point try from the second overtime and the two-point shootout from the third were adopted in 2021 (NCAA, April 2021). Earlier formats (shootout from the fifth overtime in 2019–2020; mandatory try from the third before 2021) are unverified — check that season's book.
