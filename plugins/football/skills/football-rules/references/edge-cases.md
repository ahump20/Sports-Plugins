# Edge cases, worked step by step

Every scenario below is a constructed hypothetical that illustrates a rule. None describes a real game unless it says so. "A" is the offense or kicking team; "B" is the defense or receiving team. Rule numbers are from the 2026 NFL and 2026 NCAA books.

## 1. Fumble out of the opponent's end zone

**Hypothetical:** A's runner fumbles forward at B's 2. The ball rolls through B's end zone and over the end line, and nobody recovers it.

1. The ball went out of bounds behind B's goal line on A's impetus (the fumble).
2. Result: **touchback; B's ball at its own 20.** NFL 8-7-3 Item 4(a) with 11-6. NCAA 7-2-4-c with 8-6-1 (touchback at the 20; 8-6-2).
3. Contrast: a forward fumble that goes out of bounds **in the field of play** comes back to the spot of the fumble, and A keeps the ball (NFL 8-7-3; NCAA 7-2-4-b).

Proposals to change this outcome come up most years. Check the current book before saying it's still the rule.

## 2. Fourth-down and late-game fumbles

**Hypothetical:** On 4th and 6, A's runner fumbles forward at B's 10. A teammate (not the fumbler) recovers in B's end zone.

| | NFL | NCAA |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | On 4th down, if a teammate other than the fumbler recovers, the ball goes back to the spot of the fumble, or to the spot of recovery if that is behind it (8-7-5) | Same idea: fourth-down fumble recovered by a teammate other than the fumbler (7-2-2-a Exc. 2; ball dead, 4-1-3-j) |
| Result here | No touchdown. Ball at the 10, short of the line to gain, so it goes over on downs | Same |
| Fumbler recovers his own fumble | May advance; touchdown stands | May advance |
| Defense recovers | May advance on any down | May advance |
| Same play on 2nd down, after the two-minute warning | **Same restriction applies** (8-7-6): after the two-minute warning of either half, only the fumbler can recover and advance for A | **No restriction.** NCAA has no two-minute fumble rule; the teammate's recovery and touchdown stand |

The NCAA restriction also covers fumbles on a try (4-1-3-j). For how the NFL handles fumbles on tries, check 8-7-5 and 11-3.

## 3. Muff vs fumble on a punt

**Hypothetical A:** B's returner tries to catch a punt beyond the line, but it bounces off his hands. A's gunner falls on it.
- That was a **muff**: touching without gaining possession.
- A may recover a muffed scrimmage kick beyond the line but **may not advance** it. A's ball at the recovery spot (NFL 9-3-2; NCAA 6-3-6 for recovery by the kicking team).

**Hypothetical B:** The returner catches the punt, takes two steps, and gets hit. The ball comes out and an A player scoops it up and runs.
- The returner had possession, so this is a **fumble**.
- A may advance. A touchdown stands.

The key question every time: did the receiver ever gain possession? If not, it's a muff and the kicking team can't advance it.

## 4. Safety vs touchback: impetus

Rule: the team whose **impetus** put the ball behind a goal line is responsible for it. Responsible for the ball behind your own goal line, and it dies there or goes out: **safety**. The opponent responsible: **touchback** (NFL 3-16, 11-5-1, 11-6-1; NCAA 8-5-1, 8-6-1, 8-7). NCAA adds: "When in question, it is a touchback, not a safety" (8-5-1).

| Hypothetical | Impetus | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A's runner fumbles at his own 3; the ball rolls back through A's end zone and out the end line | A's fumble | **Safety** (2 points for B) |
| B punts; the ball bounces off A's returner at A's 2 and rolls into A's end zone, where A falls on it | B's kick. Touching or deflecting a kick, or being struck by it, adds no new impetus | **Touchback** (NCAA A.R. 8-7-2-III; NFL check 3-16) |
| Same punt is rolling at A's 3 and an A player **kicks or bats** it into his own end zone and out | New impetus by A | **Safety**, plus an illegal-kick foul (NCAA 8-7-2-b; A.R. 8-7-2-II) |
| A intercepts at A's 3 and his **momentum** carries him into his own end zone, where he is downed | A's interception return | **Not a safety.** Ball to A at the spot of the interception (NCAA 8-5-1-a Exc., applies between the 5 and the goal line). NFL has a momentum exception too; check 11-5-1 for its yard limit |
| A's passer, standing in his own end zone, grounds the ball intentionally | A's foul | **Safety** (NFL 8-2-1). NCAA: grounding is loss of down at the spot of the foul (7-3-2 f–h), and a spot in A's end zone gives a safety (8-5-1-b) |

## 5. Fair catch kick

**Hypothetical:** With 0:03 left in the first half, B fair-catches a punt at A's 42.

1. B may choose to put the ball in play with a **fair catch kick**: a free kick at the goal posts from the spot of the catch.
2. Mechanics (NCAA 2-16-11, new in 2026):
   - The head coach elects it before the ready signal, and can't take it back.
   - A catch in a side zone is moved to the nearest hash mark; the kicking team can't choose another spot.
   - It is a place kick with no tee, held, or a drop kick.
   - A lines up at least 10 yards away.
   - The game clock starts when the ball is kicked.
3. NFL: 10-2-4, 11-4-3. Same idea; no tee.
4. Three points if it goes through. The kick is from about A's 42, so the attempt is roughly 52 yards: 42 plus 10 yards of end zone, with no 7-yard snap.
5. **Time:** if time expired on the punt, the period is extended for the fair catch kick (NCAA 3-2-3-a-4; NFL 4-8-2).
6. NCAA: a missed fair catch kick follows missed-field-goal rules for the next snap (2-16-11).

## 6. Illegal forward pass beyond the line

**Hypothetical:** A's quarterback scrambles, crosses the line of scrimmage by two yards, and throws forward for a completion.

1. NCAA: a forward pass is illegal if the passer's **entire body** is beyond the neutral zone when the ball is released (7-3-2-a). NFL: the same concept, under 8-1-2. Confirm the exact NFL wording there.
2. Penalty: **5 yards from the spot of the pass, plus loss of down**, in both codes (NFL 8-1-2; NCAA 7-3-2). The defense will normally accept, wiping out the completion.
3. A second forward pass on the same down is also illegal (NCAA 7-3-2-d, same penalty; NFL 8-1-2). So is a forward pass thrown from behind the line after the runner and the ball have been beyond it (NCAA 7-3-2-e).
4. A backward pass (a lateral) is legal anywhere.

## 7. Double fouls, and the NFL 15-vs-5 exception

**Hypothetical:** On a run play, A commits illegal formation (5 yards, no automatic first down or loss of down), and B commits a face mask (15 yards plus an automatic first down).

| | NFL | NCAA |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | Offsetting fouls replay the down, **except** a 15-yard foul against a 5-yard foul: the 15 is enforced and the 5 is ignored (14-5-1). The exception requires that the 5-yard foul carry no automatic first down, loss of down or clock runoff, and not be a spot foul | Offsetting fouls replay the down (10-1-4). **No 15-vs-5 exception** |
| Result | Enforce B's face mask: 15 yards and a first down | Offset. Replay the down from the previous spot |

**Change of possession on the same play** (clean hands): when both teams foul and possession changes, the team that gained the ball keeps it if it did not foul before gaining possession; otherwise the fouls offset (NFL 14-5-2; NCAA 10-1-4 and its exceptions). Check the exceptions before ruling.

## 8. Half the distance

**Hypothetical:** A has the ball at its own 6 and commits offensive holding (10 yards from the spot or previous spot).

1. A full 10 yards from the 6 would go past the goal line.
2. **No distance penalty may move the ball more than half the distance to the offender's goal line** (NFL 14-2-1; NCAA 10-2-6). Enforced from the 6, the penalty is half the distance: ball at the 3.
3. **Holding in the end zone** is a safety in both codes, not a half-distance enforcement. Check NFL 12-1-3 and NCAA 10-2-2 for the enforcement spot.
4. **NCAA exceptions** (10-2-6): defensive pass interference on scrimmage downs isn't limited this way, since it is enforced as written in 7-3-8. On a try snapped from on or inside the 3, defensive PI is enforced half the distance (10-2-5-b).

## 9. Untimed down at the end of a half

**Hypothetical:** 0:04 left in the 2nd quarter. A snaps, and time expires during the play. A flag is down for **offensive holding**, and the play gained nothing.

| | NFL | NCAA |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | An accepted **defensive** live-ball foul lets the offense choose an untimed down (4-8-2-a). An offensive foul gives **no extension**, and an offensive score on that play doesn't count (4-8-2-b). The defense may request an extension only for the listed offensive fouls: illegal touching of a kick, kick-catch or fair-catch interference, a palpably unfair act, certain personal or unsportsmanlike fouls before a change of possession or a failed 4th down, and kicking-team fouls before the receivers secure the ball | Extension for an accepted live-ball foul by **either team** (3-2-3-a-1). The offended team may refuse the extension only when the offense's foul includes loss of down. Holding doesn't |
| B accepts the holding penalty | Half ends; no untimed down for an ordinary offensive foul | Untimed down, after 10 yards are enforced |
| What B should do | Accept or decline; the half is over either way | **Decline** the penalty and the half ends. Accepting gives A another snap |

Reverse case: a **defensive** foul on the last play. Both codes extend the half with one untimed down if A accepts the penalty. NFL double fouls on the last down also extend the half, with exceptions in 4-8-2-h: both fouls dead-ball; a 5-vs-15 where the 15 is the offense's or is a dead-ball foul; the defense's only foul is dead-ball.

## 10. Tuck rule (history; repealed 2013)

- **Rule as it stood:** once the passer's arm started forward, any loss of the ball was treated as a forward pass, **even if the passer lost it while tucking it back to his body**. A ball on the ground was then an incomplete pass, not a fumble.
- The best-known application is the January 2002 AFC divisional playoff, Raiders at Patriots, where it was ruled an incomplete pass rather than a fumble.
- **Repeal:** NFL owners voted 29-1 with 2 abstentions in March 2013 (Buffalo Bills report of the owners' vote).
- **Since 2013:** if the passer loses control while bringing the ball back toward his body, it is a fumble.
- **NCAA** treats this through its own pass/fumble definitions. The NFL tuck rule never applied there.

## 11. What is a catch: NFL since 2018 vs NCAA

**NFL** (3-2-7; current wording dates from the 2018 rewrite, approved 32-0 on March 27, 2018). A receiver must:
1. secure control of the ball in his hands or arms before it touches the ground;
2. touch the ground inbounds with both feet or with any other part of his body except his hands; and
3. after (1) and (2), perform a football act (a third step, tucking the ball away, extending it toward the line), or keep control long enough to do so.

The 2018 rewrite removed the "survive the ground" requirement for a receiver going to the ground, and it allowed slight movement of the ball if the receiver keeps control.

**NCAA** (2-4-3). A receiver must:
1. secure firm control with the hands or arms before the ball touches the ground;
2. touch the ground inbounds with **any** part of the body (one foot is enough); and
3. keep control long enough to perform an act common to the game.

Unlike the NFL, a receiver **going to the ground in the act of catching** must keep complete control **through** contact with the ground. If control is lost and the ball touches the ground, it is incomplete (2-4-3-b). Losing control at the same moment the body touches the ground, or doubt about it, means incomplete (2-4-3-c). Slight movement of a controlled ball isn't a loss of possession.

**The same play can be a catch in college and not in the NFL**, and vice versa:
- One foot down at the sideline: NCAA catch, NFL incomplete.
- A receiver who secures the ball, gets both feet down, and loses it on hitting the ground: may be an NFL catch if the football-act element was met, and incomplete in NCAA.

## 12. Roughing the passer and head contact

- **NFL** (12-2-11):
  - Roughing covers forcible contact with a passer after the ball is thrown when the defender could have avoided it.
  - It includes forcible blows to the head or neck, landing on the passer with most of the defender's body weight, and forcible hits at or below the knee. Check 12-2-11 for the exact list.
  - Penalty: 15 yards and an automatic first down.
  - Helmet and defenseless-player contact on other players falls under 12-2-9 and 12-2-10 instead.
  - **No targeting rule and no automatic ejection.** A player is disqualified only for a flagrant act. The league can fine or suspend afterward.
- **NCAA** (9-1-9):
  - Roughing the passer: 15 yards plus an automatic first down. On a completed pass whose run ends beyond the neutral zone, the penalty is added to the end of the run.
  - Contact with the passer's head or neck that meets the **targeting** standard (9-1-3, 9-1-4) is also targeting: replay must confirm it, and the player is disqualified.
  - 2026 trial: a first targeting foul disqualifies for the rest of the game only. Check 9-1-4 for later offenses.
- One hit can be both roughing and targeting in college. In the NFL it is roughing, and maybe an unnecessary-roughness or defenseless-player foul, but never targeting.

## 13. Horse-collar and hip-drop tackles

- **Horse-collar:**
  - NFL (12-2-16): grabbing the inside collar of the back or side of the shoulder pads or jersey, or the jersey at the name plate or above, and pulling the runner toward the ground. It is a foul if his knees buckle, even if he isn't pulled all the way down. 15 yards and an automatic first down. It does not apply to a runner in the pocket area or in close-line play.
  - NCAA (9-1-15): 15 yards. Since 2024 it applies inside the tackle box too.
- **Hip-drop tackle:**
  - NFL (12-2-18; approved unanimously March 25, 2024): a defender grabs or wraps the runner and unweights himself, swiveling and dropping his hips onto or below the runner's legs at or below the knee.
  - Penalty: 15 yards and an automatic first down.
  - **NCAA:** no named hip-drop foul was found in the 2026 book. Such tackles are judged under the general personal-foul rules (unverified — check 9-1).

## 14. Tush push status (as of 2026)

- **NFL:**
  - Pushing a runner forward is legal; pulling a runner is a foul (12-1-4).
  - In 2025, a proposal to ban pushing the runner immediately after the snap fell short of the 24 votes it needed.
  - No similar proposal was on the 2026 agenda, so the play remains legal for 2026.
- **NCAA:** pushing the runner is legal; pulling him is a 5-yard foul (9-3-2; A.R. 9-3-2-I).
